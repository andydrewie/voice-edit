# Contextual editing signals

Use this reference for audits, heavy rewrites, or drafts with repeated stylistic friction. These are signals to investigate, not proof of AI authorship and not universal errors. A writer's sample, the publication's house style, genre conventions, and deliberate rhetorical choices take priority.

## Inflated framing

Look for claims whose intensity exceeds their evidence: grand significance, universal importance, inevitable outcomes, or a routine fact presented as a turning point. In Audit mode, diagnose the gap. In Edit mode, remove only non-substantive framing; preserve and flag a substantive claim or inference unless the user authorizes claim-level changes. Keep emphasis when the source actually earns it.

Watch for anonymous authority such as unnamed experts, reports, or consensus. Preserve a named citation. If no source exists, flag the attribution rather than inventing one.

## Delayed substance

Openers sometimes announce honesty, importance, surprise, or what readers supposedly misunderstand before stating the point. Test whether removing the setup makes the passage clearer. Keep personal context when it creates real stakes, tension, or voice.

A conclusion may repeat the preceding section without adding a decision, implication, or next step. Prefer the last concrete point. Do not force a tidy summary when the genre needs reflection or synthesis.

## Manufactured drama

Check repeated contrast formulas, question-answer pairs, staged reveals, isolated sentence fragments, and mic-drop endings. They become distracting when they substitute cadence for reasoning. Rewrite the underlying proposition plainly, but keep a dramatic construction that is distinctive, proportionate, and effective.

Colons, dashes, parentheses, fragments, and one-sentence paragraphs are normal tools. Edit clusters or monotonous dependence on them, not the marks themselves.

## Generic abstraction

Abstract nouns and weak verb phrases can hide who acted, what changed, and why it matters. Where the source contains the information, restore the actor, action, object, and result. Never manufacture a metric or example merely to make a sentence more concrete.

Do not replace canonical terminology with a casual synonym. Repeating the correct term is usually better than rotating among near-synonyms that blur meaning.

## Unsupported interpretation

Trailing commentary may announce that a fact "shows," "signals," or "underscores" a broader conclusion that the evidence does not establish. Preserve the interpretation when it is attributed or reasoned. Otherwise diagnose it; do not silently qualify or remove a substantive inference in Edit mode.

Do not add emotional reactions, confidence, personal taste, or lived experience in an attempt to create personality. Voice must come from the user's draft, instructions, or supplied sample.

## Mechanical symmetry

Repeated sentence lengths, identical paragraph shapes, serial three-part lists, matching section templates, and uniformly polished transitions can flatten a real voice. Vary structure only where the content benefits. Parallel structure is valuable when it clarifies a comparison, sequence, or set of requirements.

Formatting should support scanning and hierarchy. Check decorative bold, emoji headings, tiny sections, and unnecessary bullets, but preserve conventions required by the destination or deliberately chosen by the writer.

## Filler and fashionable vocabulary

Question any word or phrase that adds ceremony without changing the claim. There is no banned-word list: a supposedly overused word can still be the precise term, part of a quotation, or characteristic of the writer. Replace it only when a simpler expression is more accurate in context.

Hedges are not automatically filler. Preserve language that encodes probability, uncertainty, scope, politeness, or the writer's honest stance.

## Audit threshold

Report a signal only when you can explain its effect in this draft. Prefer a few high-impact clusters over exhaustive counting. If a construction works, leave it alone.
